Every year, tens of thousands of founders across Africa build products that solve real, painful, continent-scale problems. And every year, the overwhelming majority of them lose investor conversations not because their idea is weak — but because the story they tell is broken. The pitch deck is not a formality. It is the most high-stakes piece of communication your business will ever produce. It must do the work of an entire boardroom meeting in the time it takes an investor to drink a cup of coffee. Sequoia Capital — the legendary Silicon Valley venture firm behind Apple, Google, WhatsApp, Stripe, Airbnb, and YouTube — has funded companies that collectively represent over $1.4 trillion in public market capitalization. Their institutional pitch deck framework is not a set of slide templates. It is a cognitive map of exactly how world-class investors evaluate opportunity. Master this framework and you do not just build a better pitch deck. You build a better business. This is the definitive playbook.

## Slide 1: Company Purpose — The North Star That Everything Else Orbits

Sequoia does not ask for a mission statement. They ask for a company purpose — and there is a profound difference. A mission statement is inward-facing: it describes what your organization does. A company purpose is outward-facing: it articulates the singular, irreducible reason the world is better because your company exists.

Your purpose statement should be one crisp, declarative sentence. It should require zero industry jargon. A child and a pension fund manager should both understand it instantly. Amazon's early purpose was not 'to operate an online bookstore' — it was 'to be Earth's most customer-centric company.' Google's was not 'to build a search engine' — it was 'to organize the world's information and make it universally accessible and useful.' Notice how both statements describe an ambitious, almost audacious outcome, not a product feature.

For African founders, the purpose statement carries extra weight because your market context is less familiar to most institutional investors. Your purpose must simultaneously communicate the scale of what you are building and dissolve any unconscious hesitation an investor might carry about the African market. If you are building cold-chain logistics in West Africa, do not say 'we are a last-mile delivery company.' Say: 'We exist to ensure that no African farmer loses a harvest before it reaches a customer.' That sentence carries urgency, moral weight, and commercial logic simultaneously.

> Your company purpose is not what you do. It is the version of the world that justifies your company's existence. Investors fund futures, not features.

## Slide 2: Problem — Make the Investor Feel the Pain Before They See the Solution

The single biggest mistake founders make in their pitch deck is rushing to the solution. They are so excited about what they have built that they skip past the problem — the very thing that makes their solution necessary. Sequoia insists that the problem slide must make investors viscerally feel the pain before a single word of solution is spoken.

A great problem slide does three things simultaneously. First, it establishes that the problem is real and not manufactured — grounding its existence in data, observable behavior, or lived experience. Second, it quantifies the economic cost of the problem's existence, answering the implicit investor question: 'Why does this matter financially?' Third, it reveals that existing solutions are inadequate — creating the logical vacuum that your company uniquely fills.

African founders have an extraordinary advantage on this slide: your problems are undeniable and your market is underserved by incumbents who lack local knowledge. The cost of post-harvest crop loss across sub-Saharan Africa exceeds $48 billion annually. The African SME financing gap stands at $331 billion. Over 600 million Africans live without reliable electricity. These are not niche problems — they are civilizational opportunities dressed as crises. But resist the temptation to overwhelm with statistics. One sharp, human number communicated with emotional resonance will always outperform a slide crowded with data tables that investors scan and forget.

> If investors do not feel the urgency of the problem by the end of Slide 2, no amount of brilliant solution design will rescue your pitch.

## Slide 3: Solution — Clarity, Not Complexity. Show the Elegance.

After the problem has landed with weight, the solution slide must arrive with a feeling of inevitability. The best solution slides feel less like a product demo and more like: 'Of course. Why didn't someone do this sooner?' That sensation — the 'why didn't anyone do this?' feeling — is a signal that you have found genuine product-market fit, not just a feature set.

Sequoia's guidance is deliberately disciplined: describe your solution in concrete, non-jargon language. Do not confuse sophistication of technology with the quality of your pitch. The most complex AI systems in the world are pitched to investors using the simplest possible language. If you need three paragraphs to explain your product on a slide, you do not yet understand your product well enough. The classic test: can you explain your solution to a 12-year-old? Can you explain the value it creates to a 70-year-old pension fund manager? If both can understand it, you are ready.

Critically, the solution slide is not your product roadmap. You are not listing features — you are communicating a mechanism: the core engine by which your company creates value. Show how the problem and the solution snap together like two puzzle pieces. Every word on this slide should exist only to prove that your solution is the minimum necessary intervention that resolves the maximum amount of pain.

## Slide 4: Why Now — The Most Underestimated Slide in Every African Pitch

Every investor who has been in the game long enough has seen brilliant ideas pitched at the wrong time — and watched them fail. They have also seen mediocre ideas succeed because a timing tailwind carried them forward. The 'Why Now' slide is where you demonstrate to investors that this is not just a good idea — it is a good idea whose time has irreversibly arrived.

The 'Why Now' slide answers a deceptively dangerous question: 'If this problem has existed for years, why has no one solved it yet — and why will you succeed now where others have not?' Your answer must point to at least one of four structural triggers: a technological shift (new infrastructure, APIs, or platforms that did not exist two years ago), a regulatory change (new laws, AfCFTA trade corridors, or policy frameworks that create a legal pathway), a behavioral shift (pandemic-accelerated digital adoption, demographic youth bulge, urbanization), or an economic unlock (a collapsing cost curve, new capital availability, or a market liquidity event).

For African founders in 2026, the 'Why Now' argument is extraordinarily powerful when constructed correctly. The African Continental Free Trade Area (AfCFTA) represents $3.4 trillion in combined GDP — the world's largest free trade zone by country count. Mobile internet penetration across the continent crossed 50% in 2024 and is accelerating. The African Development Bank has committed $25 billion to infrastructure finance through 2030. The demographic reality is unavoidable: Africa will be home to 25% of the world's working-age population by 2035. Investors who ignore Africa today are not being prudent — they are being historically shortsighted.

> 'Why Now' is the slide where founders prove timing mastery. Sequoia's greatest investments were not just great ideas — they were great ideas arriving at the precise moment the world became ready to receive them.

## Slide 5: Market Size — TAM, SAM, SOM and Why Most Founders Get This Wrong

The market size slide is where the most ambitious founders and the most intellectually dishonest founders diverge. Sequoia is extraordinarily specific about this: they do not want a top-down market size that begins with 'Africa's GDP is $3 trillion and we are capturing 0.1%.' That approach is universally unconvincing because it requires no business logic — any company in any market can make that claim.

Instead, Sequoia expects a bottom-up market sizing built from first-principles logic: How many customers exist who have the problem you solve? What is the maximum price each of them would rationally pay for your solution? Multiply those two numbers together and you have your Serviceable Addressable Market (SAM). Work upward from there to establish your Total Addressable Market (TAM) by expanding the customer universe. Then work downward to define your Serviceable Obtainable Market (SOM) — the realistic slice you can capture in a defined time horizon given your go-to-market capacity.

Present your market size across three lenses: your initial beachhead market (where you win first), your expansion market (adjacent opportunities you unlock after product-market fit), and your long-term platform market (the ecosystem you eventually anchor). Sequoia backs companies that start in small, specific markets and expand to enormous ones — not companies that try to boil the ocean from day one. Amazon started with books. Google started with academic search queries. Uber started with black-car service in San Francisco. What is your beachhead? Define it with ruthless precision.

## Slide 6: Product — Demonstrate, Do Not Describe. Investors Trust Evidence.

By Slide 6, investors should already believe the problem is real, the solution is elegant, the timing is right, and the market is enormous. The product slide is where belief converts into conviction. This is where you show — not tell — what you have built.

Sequoia's preference is sharp: show the product in action through a live demo, a video walkthrough, annotated screenshots, or a before/after user journey map. Never use a slide full of bullet points to describe a product that could instead be demonstrated in 60 seconds of screen recording. The product slide answers a fundamental investor question: 'Can this team actually build what they are promising?' Evidence of a working product — even a minimal, early-stage version — is exponentially more convincing than the most beautifully designed concept presentation.

For early-stage African founders who may not yet have a polished consumer product, the product slide can be anchored in customer proof instead of software polish. Show your first pilot customers. Show a screenshot of your first WhatsApp order bot taking a real transaction. Show a photograph of your first cold-chain container being loaded. Real evidence of real commercial activity is worth more than perfect design mock-ups, because it signals founder resourcefulness — one of the traits Sequoia prizes above almost everything else.

> A product that exists, even imperfectly, closes more investment rounds than a perfect product that only lives inside a Figma prototype.

## Slide 7: Team — Why You? The Most Loaded Question in Every Investment Room

Sequoia has a famous internal mantra: 'We back missionaries, not mercenaries.' The team slide is where investors evaluate whether the founders are the specific people on Earth best positioned to solve this specific problem. It is also — quietly — the slide where investors make their most visceral, emotional judgments about whether they want to spend the next 7 to 10 years in a business relationship with you.

Great team slides do not list credentials for their own sake. They tell a story of unique founder-market fit: Why is this the right team for this problem at this time? Have you lived the problem personally? Do you carry domain expertise that would take a competitor years to replicate? Have you already demonstrated execution capability — shipping product, closing customers, navigating regulatory complexity — in contexts that directly mirror the challenge you are tackling?

For African founders, the team slide is a critical opportunity to neutralize 'implicit risk' in the investor's mind. International investors often carry unconscious assumptions about the operational difficulty of building in African markets. Your team slide should feature advisors, corporate partners, and institutional validators who lend credibility to your local execution capacity. A board member who is a former central bank official, a strategic investor who is a respected regional conglomerate, or an advisory relationship with a global consulting firm all communicate that your team operates within serious institutional networks — not in isolation.

## Slide 8: Business Model — Show How Every Dollar You Earn Is Structurally Defensible

The business model slide is where story converts into arithmetic. Sequoia needs to understand — with crystal clarity — how your company makes money, how that revenue scales with growth, and why customers will continue paying you over time. This slide is not about complexity. The best business models can be explained in two sentences. The complexity lies in the economics, not the mechanism.

For each revenue stream, answer four questions: Who pays? What triggers payment? What is the unit price? And what is the contribution margin after direct costs? Then demonstrate the scalability thesis: what happens to your unit economics as you grow from 100 customers to 10,000? Does the gross margin expand as you scale (a positive signal), contract (a danger sign that requires explanation), or remain stable? Sequoia particularly favors business models with built-in network effects — where each additional customer makes the product more valuable for all existing customers — and recurring revenue structures like subscriptions and transaction fees that compound investor returns.

African market context creates some important nuances here. Your pricing must reflect the purchasing power realities of your primary market without undercutting your long-term capital efficiency. Show investors that you have done the work: you understand your customer's ability to pay, you have priced for sustainable unit economics, and you have a credible pathway to margin expansion as operational density increases across your market geography.

## Slide 9: Competition — Do Not Hide From Competitors. Dominate the Narrative About Them.

Every pitch deck has a competition slide. The vast majority of them are strategic disasters. Founders either claim they have 'no competition' — instantly destroying their credibility — or they populate a feature comparison matrix that buries investors in details without conveying a clear competitive advantage. Sequoia's approach is more sophisticated: they want to understand not who your competitors are today, but what competitive position you are constructing that will be difficult to replicate in the future.

Position your competitive landscape around two or three axes that reveal a white space only you occupy. Choose axes that are genuinely strategically important to your customer — not axes you have engineered to make yourself look good. The worst competition slides weaponize irrelevant metrics to manufacture artificial superiority. Sophisticated investors can spot this immediately, and it destroys the trust you have spent seven slides building.

Your most important job on the competition slide is to answer: 'Why won't an incumbent simply copy what you are doing once you have proven the market?' This is the question of defensibility — your moat. Moats in African markets can be built from operational depth (the years it takes to master supply chain relationships across a regional corridor), regulatory positioning (being first to obtain a critical license that creates barriers to entry), network effects (a marketplace that becomes more liquid with each additional participant), or proprietary data (behavioral patterns accumulated from direct customer engagement that no late entrant can replicate). Name your moat explicitly. Investors do not reward modesty here.

> Saying you have no competitors does not signal a blue ocean. It signals that you have not done the research — and that is a far more dangerous impression to leave in an investment room.

## Slide 10: Financials — The Language Investors Think In. Speak It Fluently.

The financials slide is where the narrative you have built across nine slides must be validated by numerical architecture. Sequoia expects a 3-to-5 year financial projection: revenue, gross profit, operating expenses, EBITDA (or operating loss), and monthly cash burn. They also want to see key operational metrics that drive your financial model — Monthly Active Users, Average Order Value, Customer Acquisition Cost, Lifetime Value, Gross Merchandise Volume, or whatever the North Star metric is for your specific business model.

The cardinal sin of financial projections is the 'hockey stick' — exponential growth from Year 1 that has no mechanistic explanation. Investors have seen thousands of hockey stick projections. They do not find them inspiring; they find them evasive. Your projections must be built bottom-up from real assumptions: specific marketing spend, projected conversion rates from your channel mix, average deal size based on real customer data, and headcount growth tied directly to operational milestones. Every projection should be defensible when challenged. If an investor asks 'How did you arrive at this revenue number for Year 3?' you must be able to walk them through the arithmetic, layer by layer.

Always close the financials slide with the current ask: the precise amount of capital you are raising, the primary use of proceeds broken into major categories (product development, market expansion, team hires, working capital), and the specific milestone this capital is engineered to achieve. The milestone should be a clearly defined inflection point that meaningfully de-risks the business for the next round of investors. Show investors not just where their money goes — show them what it transforms. That transformation is the final emotional anchor of your entire pitch.
